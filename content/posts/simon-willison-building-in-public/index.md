Earlier this year, Simon Willison — one of the most respected voices in the AI developer community — quoted something I wrote on his blog. It was a single line from my post about microservices, and it changed how I think about building in public.

Here's what happened, and the three lessons I took from it.

## The Context

I published a post on nexo.sh arguing that microservices are a tax most startups can't afford. It was an opinion I'd developed over years of consulting with early-stage startups, watching them over-engineer their architectures. I posted it, shared it on DEV Community, and moved on.

A few days later, Simon Willison included a quote from my post in his daily link blog — a curated feed read by tens of thousands of developers. The traffic spike was nice, but the real value was validation: **an industry authority found my thinking worth sharing.**

## Lesson 1: Write for Experts, Not Beginners

The post that caught Simon's attention wasn't a tutorial. It wasn't "How to Set Up Microservices with Docker." It was an opinionated take backed by real-world experience. Experts don't share beginner content. They share perspectives that make them think.

If you want industry recognition, write the post that makes a senior engineer nod and forward it to their team. Not the one that explains what a REST API is.

## Lesson 2: Consistency Beats Virality

Simon Willison publishes every single day. His blog is a daily practice, not a marketing campaign. The reason he found my post is that he reads widely and consistently. The reason his endorsement carries weight is that he's been doing this for years.

Building in public isn't about going viral once. It's about showing up consistently until the compounding effects kick in. One post gets picked up. That drives traffic to your other posts. Those posts build your reputation. That reputation opens doors.

## Lesson 3: Your Unique Angle Is Your Unfair Advantage

There are thousands of people writing about microservices. My post stood out because it came from a specific perspective: a technical leader who's seen the pattern fail repeatedly at startups. That specificity is what made it quotable.

Your unique angle isn't "I know about AI." It's the intersection of your specific experience, your technical depth, and your willingness to take a clear position. Find that intersection and write from it relentlessly.

## Applying This Going Forward

I'm now building agentpack — a package manager for AI agents — and documenting the entire journey publicly. Every technical decision, every architecture choice, every lesson learned. Not because I expect every post to go viral, but because consistent, expert-level content compounds.

The AI agent space is moving fast. There's room for thoughtful, experienced voices who can separate signal from noise. If you're building in this space, start writing about it today. The compounding clock starts when you publish your first post.
